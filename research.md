I have a few-many more years left in my PhD journey, but these are my main focal research projects thus far

### Identifying Resistance Genes in Phaseolus vulgaris

In 1923, Karl Sax demonstrated how natural genetic variation could be used to link traits to genomic regions, laying the groundwork for modern QTL mapping. As a young scientist, I am incredibly excited by the novel technologies available to study biological systems, so I initially wondered whether this project would limit my research to traditional plant breeding. Instead, I have come to appreciate the enduring power of QTL mapping as a tool for scientific discovery.

I find it exciting that a methodology rooted in nearly a century of research can still help us uncover something entirely new. By combining QTL mapping with modern molecular and genomic approaches, I hope to identify the genes underlying nematode resistance in common bean. To me, this project represents what makes science so exciting: building on the discoveries of the past while using today's technologies to answer questions that remain unresolved.

### Parasitic Nematode - Host Metabolomic Interactions 

Plant Parasitic Nematodes (PPNs) are a diverse class a tiny worms that infect and feed off of plant tissue to survive. I study Meloidogyne incognita, an obligate PPN that establishes itself within plant roots and remains sedentary throughout the remainder of its life cycle.

Nematodes require essential nutrients that they cannot produce themselves to survive and reproduce. Through M.incognita's evolutionary dependence on plant hosts, they have lost or never developed the ability to synthesize certain essential nutrients themselves. My research aims to identify these missing metabolic pathways and determine which nutrients M. incognita acquires from its host, addressing fundamental questions about parasite metabolism and host dependence that remain incompletely understood.
