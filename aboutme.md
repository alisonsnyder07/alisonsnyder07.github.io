My name is Alison Snyder. I am a PhD student at UC Davis studying plant biology, focusing and the genetic and metabolic interactions between plant parasitic nematodes and their broad host range. The ultimate goal of my research is to identify different avenues to induce resistance against these agriculturally detrimental parasites that effect global agriculture 

### My story

I was born and raised in the San Francisco Bay Area, California, surrounded by a culture of technology, innovation, and possibility. Growing up in this environment shaped my belief that scientific discovery can push boundaries and lead to meaningful change. That belief continues to guide my approach to research and my interest in developing more sustainable agricultural systems.

My academic journey began at California Polytechnic State University, San Luis Obispo, where I studied Environmental Earth and Soil Sciences before transitioning to Biology. My early research focused on soil biogeochemistry and nutrient cycling in agricultural systems, particularly in orchard soils. Through this work, I developed an appreciation for the complexity of agricultural ecosystems and the fundamental role they play in sustaining human life. I became increasingly interested in how we could make these systems more productive and sustainable.

As I progressed through my undergraduate coursework, I discovered molecular biology, genetics, and biochemistry. Although I remained fascinated by soil systems and their ecological complexity, I became increasingly interested in the possibilities of biotechnology to address agricultural challenges. I wanted to understand not only how plants interact with their environments, but also the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying those interactions.

After graduating in 2023, I joined a postbaccalaureate research program through the San Diego Botanic Garden and the Salk Institute. This experience allowed me to bridge my background in environmental science with my growing interest in plant genetics. I investigated salt tolerance in Phaseolus filiformis, a wild relative of the common bean, exploring variation in stress tolerance across genetically diverse plant accessions. This project introduced me to plant genetic resources and the potential of crop wild relatives to inform agricultural improvement.

I subsequently worked as a Research Associate at the Salk Institute in Lena Mueller's lab, where I investigated plant-beneficial arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi across diverse legume species. Using approaches spanning phenotyping, microscopy, population genetics, and comparative genomics, I explored variation in plant–microbe interactions and the evolutionary patterns that may shape them. These experiences strengthened my interest in understanding plant traits through the combined lenses of genetics, evolution, and ecology.

After two years of professional research experience, I knew graduate school was the next step in developing as a scientist. I wanted to build on my background in plant–microbe interactions using novel techniques like spatial -omics and CRISPr while deepening my expertise in molecular biology and computational approaches. Just as importantly, I wanted to pursue questions that could connect fundamental biological mechanisms with practical agricultural applications.

I am now a second-year PhD student in Shahid Siddique's lab at UC Davis, where I study plant resistance to plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN). One of my projects focuses on identifying genetic resistance to nematodes in common bean using quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping and molecular biology techniques. In a complementary project, I investigate the genomic and metabolic mechanisms involved in nutrient acquisition and interactions between plant-parasitic nematodes and their diverse host plants.

I wanted to build on my background in plant–microbe interactions while exploring emerging approaches, such as spatial omics and CRISPR-based technologies, to investigate biological questions at finer molecular and cellular scales. I also wanted to deepen my expertise in molecular biology and computational approaches, bringing these tools together to understand the mechanisms underlying plant traits and interactions. Just as importantly, I wanted to pursue research that connects fundamental biological discoveries with practical applications in sustainable agriculture.

### Beyond the Lab

Outside of research, I enjoy growing houseplants, experimenting with plant propagation and tissue culture, running, and practicing yoga. I am also passionate about science communication, teaching, and mentorship. I enjoy making complex scientific concepts accessible and helping others develop the confidence to explore questions of their own.

My path into plant science has been interdisciplinary, spanning soil systems, plant ecology, genetics, and molecular biology. I see these fields not as separate disciplines, but as connected scales of the same system: from the soil surrounding a root to the molecular pathways operating within a cell. I hope to continue working at these intersections, using advances in plant biotechnology and genomics to better understand plant biology and contribute to the future of sustainable agriculture.
